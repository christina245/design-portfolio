Case study — product design

Retrocodex

Designing an inclusive knowledge-discovery platform around a problem thousands of social media users thought was impossible to solve.

ROLE
Solo product designer
TIMELINE
Sep 2025 – Apr 2026
SCOPE
Research, IA, UX/UI, beta launch
LIVE SITE
theretrocodex.com ↗
product hero shot — 1200 × 630

I identified an internet-wide unmet user need, validated it using thousands of organic user comments, designed around seemingly contradictory user requirements, shipped an MVP, and iterated it into a product that reached millions of people.

A few individuals had attempted versions of this site before, but none gained meaningful traction.

01 — origin

Where it started

In September 2025, I came across a viral post that had been circulating online since 2020. Because of the topic's virality, several user pain points emerged — leading to a tough, seemingly impossible problem statement.

grid of popular reposts — 744 × 220

The problem

How might we design a platform that helps users discover outdated information they may have learned, while acknowledging that:

  • knowledge is not standardized
  • educational experiences differ dramatically by location
  • scientific consensus changes gradually rather than instantly
  • many facts never have an official revision date

How I addressed this, in a nutshell

  • Disclaimers that content may not reflect each user's experience and is continuously updated
  • Facts classified by graduation decade rather than year
  • Interactive "Were you taught this" polls and comments
  • A world map classifying submissions by country
  • Registered users set a location and discover a feed of local facts and comments
  • Facts labeled with the years they were widely taught
  • Facts still circulating today are tagged and surfaced on every decade view
02 — validation

From concept to reality

As the prompt circulated, users repeatedly named "facts" they were taught that were later overturned or never true — Pluto is a planet, blood is blue until it hits oxygen, Marie Antoinette's "let them eat cake."

Yet five years later, nobody had successfully built the product. That raised the real UX question: if demand is this obvious, why hasn't anyone solved it?

10,000+
organic comments analyzed

The same complaints appeared over and over:

"Education isn't standardized."
"Every country teaches different things."
"Every state teaches different things."
"Different school districts update curriculum at different times."

Most of these topics had no official date when they were overturned in curricula or public belief — so the original "input the year" idea couldn't produce accurate, inclusive results. I initially planned to classify facts by subject instead: history, life sciences, social sciences, gender & sexuality, and more.

03 — research

Research

After analyzing thousands of naturally occurring discussions, I ran my own survey with 15 people interested in the concept, focused on their experiences being taught untrue facts.

survey results overview — 744 × 260

For encyclopedic content, I researched "common misconceptions we were taught" round-ups and chose 100 of the most commonly repeated facts — leaving the rest open for user submissions.

While primary research was pending, I revisited the social discussions for what users expected from the product. Several concerns kept surfacing. Users wanted:

  • personalized results
  • regional relevance
  • trustworthy sourcing
  • discussion around nuanced topics
  • acknowledgement that people learned different things
FROM
"Build a database of outdated facts"
→
TO
"Design an inclusive system for people whose educational experiences are fundamentally varied"
04 — design challenges

Three design challenges

1. Knowledge isn't globally standardized

Most reposts sparked the same argument: "You were taught that in school? I wasn't." Or: "My kid is being taught this now." A universal fact list would inevitably alienate someone.

screenshots of comments — 744 × 180
SOLUTION

Instead of pretending every fact applied universally, I designed transparency directly into the experience. Every page explains that:

  • educational experiences vary
  • facts learned aren't guaranteed to apply to everyone
  • science continues evolving
  • new discoveries happen gradually

Rather than hiding uncertainty, the interface embraces it — shifting the product from declaring what users were taught toward helping them explore what they may have learned.

2. Most outdated facts don't have a single revision date

Many discoveries happen over decades, not overnight:

  • Nutrition guidance changes gradually — the USDA has revised its Food Pyramid three times in 35 years
  • Myths like "you only use 10% of your brain" circulated for decades after being disproven
  • Historical interpretations evolve as previously overlooked voices come to light

Assigning a single cutoff year would create false precision — the only exception being facts overturned by an official governing body, like a Food Pyramid revision or a country renaming or splitting.

DESIGN DECISION

I organized knowledge by graduation decade instead of year — an approach that also accommodates gradual curriculum change.

homepage — 744 × 220

3. Educational experiences are regional

Users consistently said: "Everything depends on where you went to school. If I went to school in South Africa, I don't expect to have learned the same facts as someone from California." Instead of treating location as metadata, I made it part of the product's social experience. Users can:

  • specify where they grew up
  • discover users nearby
  • browse local discussions
  • view a Local feed
  • follow contributors with similar educational backgrounds

The result turns Retrocodex from a static archive into a geographically contextual knowledge community.

05 — information architecture

Multiple ways in

Structuring the site by decade alone wouldn't be fully accurate — users from regions behind in curriculum updates may still be taught facts phased out elsewhere. As one respondent put it:

"I know I graduated in 2004, but since I grew up in a poorly-funded region, I think there's a lot more I learned that may be currently outdated than I can see on the results for my graduation decade."

To accommodate that, the site can be explored through multiple dimensions:

  • graduation decade
  • topic category
  • popularity
  • recently added
  • debated topics
  • current events
  • regionally taught topics

06 — trust

Building trust

The most common pain point was "I feel like I can't trust what I've been taught." Addressing it meant designing clear credibility signals rather than another fact-checking site:

  • citations on every fact
  • revision timelines
  • debate indicators
  • "Partially True" labels
  • historical context
  • nuanced explanations instead of binary right/wrong language

Outcomes

Since the beta shipped in late April 2026, Retrocodex has:

  • Appeared in viral videos totaling more than 4 million views
  • Been featured on podcasts including Grumpy Old Geeks and Modern Wisdom
  • Built an accompanying Reddit community with 6,000+ members
  • Been featured on Bored Panda

Perhaps most importantly, the project validated that a problem many believed couldn't be solved could be made approachable through careful product design — not just technical complexity.
